# North Road and Eventual Isles cast bible

Backstories and relationships for the named people beyond Pingsby, the companion to `docs/cast-bible.md` (Pingsby's fourteen). Every line written for these people should agree with what is here. Their daily schedules are in `game/js/folk-far.js`, and the relationship scenes are data in `game/js/folk-far-arcs.js`.

How the scenes play: each arc has three scenes. A scene plays when you talk to someone in it on their own map, from your second conversation with them, once nobody in it has a quest step or an Isles commission waiting. Seen scenes stay seen across loops, the arc's last scene lands its system-design analogy and offers one review question, and Bru's Brújula Board lists them with Pingsby's. Crowd extras (the arena's fans and bookie, the Exchange queue, the road's merchant, traveller, courier, pilgrim and guest, the Exchange trader) and Kyu are left out. Heroes #1023 and #500 have backstories but no arc, since the story decides where they are.

## The North Road

Backstories and relationship arcs for the named people of the North Road and its side maps: the road itself, Lord Latency's waystation, the tournament arena, the heroes' camp and the Exchange. The ground rules are Pingsby's: the game wins, show it and don't tell it, scenes are short, and only an arc's last scene lands a system design analogy. Where people stand each time of day comes from `game/js/folk-far.js`. Anything not already in the game is new, and Ladi can veto it without breaking anything.

### Marta

Marta has driven the same caravan up and down the North Road for thirty years: Pingsby's honey north, the Exchange's ledgers south, and vegetables she somehow grows in the crates. She stands by her cart at the Pingsby end of the road all day, haggles with the merchant every evening, and sleeps in the caravan. She is brisk, practical and fond of exactly one living thing, her horse, who has taken up painting since you cut her repacking down to three crates.

Her title says "Never Lost a Crate (Twice)" because she lost one, once. It was her first spring on the road, it was crate 7, and it was the good one. She has never stopped checking under the horse for it. That is why she loves crate 7 now, the one whose hash never changes: it is the crate she is sure of. What she wants is a ledger with no gaps in it, and an answer about the one that got away.

### Fen

Fen is the youngest courier on the road and has the fastest legs on it. He runs letters out of the courier post halfway up the road, and spends his evenings swapping stories there with the long-haired courier who walks the whole route, before sleeping at the post. He talks the way he runs, in short bursts, and the thing he hates most in the world is a door nobody answers: asleep, out or gone forever, he can never tell.

He was born on the road, in the back of Marta's caravan, during a snowstorm; she is the closest thing he has to an aunt, and she still counts him with her crates. He wants to be first everywhere, which means every letter he carries arrives before the thing it is about, and he is quietly proud of that. His soft spot is the letters people never open. He says he respects the two grey ticks, but he walks past those doors a little slower.

### Lyra

Lyra is writing the Ballad of Everyone: every hero, where they are from, and who fought beside whom. She warms up at her stage halfway up the road in the morning, plays through the afternoon, headlines the evening show, and goes in at night. Since you helped her file it, the ballad lives in scrolls, cards and string, and you are verse two hundred and twelve. Pip is verse two hundred and thirteen and has asked for a key change.

She left a much grander court because nobody there wanted songs about ordinary people, and she has made a whole life out of them since. The secret is that there is no verse about Lyra. She has written one for everyone who walks past, and never got round to herself. Explodia, next door, is the only person on the road loud enough to notice.

### Ren

Ren is the detective of the North Road. He sits on a milestone by the courier post because it raises his deductive reasoning by 40%, he goes barefoot because chairs are a conspiracy, and he runs on sugar cubes. Every guess comes as a percentage that he corrects out loud until it reaches 100%. He closed the Case of the Smudged Scoreboard for the Tide Pool Game Club and kept the chalkboard as evidence.

Before the road, he solved cases nobody had asked him to solve, which made him unpopular. Here, people bring him things, and he has never been happier. He doesn't accuse anyone without proof, and he wants a case worth his whole brain. His soft spot is being wrong in a nice way: Ploy sent him a mango when he gave it a 2% chance, and he still talks about it.

### Explodia

Explodia is a demolitions mage who has walked away from more explosions than anyone alive, and the least reliable person on the road, so naturally she runs the Blast Academy by the bard's stage and teaches the SRE Book. She casts one blast a day, allegedly at noon. She has ten million followers, a hat that catches fire, and a voice that only goes up. Suzu's brigade once banned her for spending the whole error budget by the tenth of the month.

After every blast she sits in the smoke and writes the postmortem in a little notebook. There are thousands of them now, and she has never shown one to anyone. What she wants is to be seen, loudly and on schedule. Her secret is that of her ten million followers, only one has ever left a comment. It is Kyu, and it always says "Nice. Fix the tail latency." It is her favourite comment.

### Gong

Gong is master of the tournament arena. He hits the gong; that is the job, and the gong sometimes goes off on its own when he gets excited ("Sorry. Reflex."). He runs the ticket booth and the fights from his spot by the stands, sleeps behind the fighters' gate, and believes the crowd deserves every punch live. The last arena master sent results by pigeon, and he is now a pigeon.

Under the noise, Gong is a gentle, shy man with enormous arms from carrying the warning gong to the road and back for Explodia every day. He secretly wants to learn the triangle. He is also in an undeclared war with President Akane over the camp's budget lock, which he spent on a bigger gong, and he rings his gong very softly whenever she is mentioned.

### Suzu

Suzu is Brigade Chief of the S.O.S. Brigade, the Service-level Objectives Society, as it says on her armband. She founded it to find aliens, time travellers and whoever actually answers the pager, and recruits anyone who stands still at the arena long enough. She stands by the ring all day and turns in at night. She found the realm's one-name on-call roster, designed a real rotation, and made Kyu an honorary member without asking her.

She was bored her whole life until she found out the Cloud had been run by one person since year 3, which is the best mystery she has ever heard. She wants the brigade to matter. Her soft spot is that Kyu sent back a sticky note that said "Thank you", and Suzu keeps it inside her armband.

### Zen

Zen is terrified of everything, including dust, and brave only when he is asleep. Asleep, he does Thunder Breathing, First Form, and strikes once, cleanly. Awake, he cries, refreshes ticket booths four thousand times, and apologises for things he did in his sleep. He stands by the far side of the ring, practising his cheer for Princess Hana's concert (seat 14, his name on it).

He grew up being told he would never amount to anything, and half believes it. What he wants is for someone to see him being good at something while he's awake. His soft spot is that he noticed something nobody else did: Kyu, after their fight, said "thank you" to him very quietly, and he doesn't think anyone ever says it back to her.

### Robert E.O. Hypewagon

Robert E.O. Hypewagon is a gentleman who explains everything, loudly, as it happens. He stands at the heroes' camp by the fire and the tents, narrates the camp to itself in the evenings, and goes in at night. He sent crawlers out to catalogue every scroll in the realm so that one day Kyu's runbooks can be found by the people who need them, and only nearly got barricaded out of the library.

He used to be a sidekick to a hero who never once thanked him, and decided that if nobody else would tell the story of the people doing the work, he would. "Somebody should have narrated Kyu's ten thousand years!" His secret is that he narrates because he is shy; he cannot say anything quietly. He needs his hat to look surprised in.

### President Akane

President Akane leads the Heroes' Camp Council and has never lost a battle in nine years, or confessed anything first. She stands by the camp notice board, thinks by the fire in the evenings, and goes in at night. She held the lock on the council budget at noon, paused for forty seconds to think, and found Gong had taken it at 12:01. She read Kyu's rejected proposal for fencing tokens in the council archives and found it unbearably elegant.

She was the youngest council president the camp has ever had and has treated every conversation as a battle since. What she wants, though she would never say it, is tea with Gong, without either of them having to go first. Her soft spot is the arena: she looks east several times a minute, and says it is the weather.

### Lord Latency

Lord Latency was the Demon King of Lag, boss of the Tidewater Archives, until you beat him in chapter 2 and he broke seventeen windows being defeated. Now he runs the waystation at the start of the northbound tunnel: tea at the tables by day, the well in the evening, the inn at night. He pays off his debt to Wen in dish shifts at the Cache Café, walks the road with you slowly, and once per fight can delay an incident's next move. He commentates at the arena, very slowly.

His army was an unbounded queue, and that is why he lost. Kyu rate-limited him to one request a year for a century; he hated her, and now he thinks she was teaching, though nobody asked her to. He would like to be useful, and is beginning to suspect that being slow is a kind of patience. His soft spot is his old castle: even the skeletons had weekends, and he misses being a good boss.

### Hero #77 and Hero #256

Two of the 1,023 heroes Uptima summoned who quit, now sparring partners at the camp's sparring ground all day, and sitting by the fire all night (#77 asleep, #256 awake). Hero #77 lasted seventy-seven loops and quit because Uptima asked him to smile in the posters. In Kyu's army he was the one holding a lunch box, and he is the one who reminds everyone that they get lunch breaks now.

Hero #256 is a power of two and will tell you so. He asked about documentation once, once, and has never forgotten the look he got. He runs the camp's rotation now with fierce, quiet care, the kind of person who reads the roster aloud every afternoon. Neither of them has ever won a sparring match against the other, and both keep count.

### Hero #3

Hero #3 was one of the first heroes Uptima ever summoned. She looked at him and said "too short for posters", so he trained. He has been punching the same training dummy at the camp for nine thousand years, sits by the fire in the evenings and goes to his tent at night. He noticed long ago that the dummy's stake is loose, and keeps saying somebody should write that down.

He wants to be a real hero, the kind in the ballad, and he is far stronger than anyone realises. His soft spot is the dummy itself: he talks to it, and he is fairly sure it says he's ready.

### Hero #12

Hero #12 was summoned, quit, and became the camp's vending machine, which is the best job he has ever had. He has been a vending machine for nine thousand years. Kyu used to throw him at Uptima, and he loved flying. He used to charge people for every press of the button, until you and Ayo gave every purchase its own ticket number, and he once turned into a retry storm when Explodia knocked over the camp ovens.

Kyu used to come by after her shift and fix him every night, and leave a sticky note: "You're doing great. K." He has kept every one of them in his coin tray. He wants to be useful, and to be pressed once.

### Hero #404

Hero #404 is not here. A sign on the ground where he stands says NOT FOUND, and it has been signed by Hero #404. Nobody at the camp has ever seen him, and everyone is sure he is there.

He was summoned, looked at the posters, and quietly decided not to be found. He likes the camp, he likes being on the rotation, and he likes lunch, which disappears when you leave it out for him. His secret is that he is the camp's most reliable member: he has never missed a shift, because nobody could ever tell.

### Hero #1023

Hero #1,023 is the one right before you. He has died a thousand and twenty-three times, and Kyu was there for most of them; he remembers that she always looked so tired. He sits by the fire at the camp from the day it opens, and he is the hero you would have been if nobody had ever come looking for her.

He quit like all the others and joined Kyu's army with a lanyard and a rotation. He doesn't resent Uptima or Kyu. What he wants is for you to finish what he couldn't, and his soft spot is that he still keeps his summoning paperwork, with his number crossed out and "next" written in Kyu's hand.

### Hero #500

Hero #500 was summoned with only a shield, and decided it was a strategy. He entered the Tournament of Power Levels, met Kyu in the bracket, charged, and was no longer in the ring. After chapter 8 he leaves the arena and sits by the fire at the camp. Kyu fixed his sword once, and then told him the sword was never the problem.

He kept the shield. A fan at the arena bet her pension on him, and he knows it, and it is the reason he still trains. What he wants is one more match, a fair one, and his soft spot is the ticket stub from that tournament, which he carries everywhere.

### Nari

Nari is the first one onto any new ground. Wherever the road opens a place, she is already there, dancing on it to show that it holds, and she gives the first person through after her a few shards for being brave, a few more each time. The veil shows about one percent of her face. She talks like she dances, nonstop and delighted with herself ("I'm my own favourite!"), counts in beats instead of seconds, narrates her own life like a film, and when a place has held she says "Rollout complete!" and is gone. Her motto is a film's: if it isn't a happy ending, it isn't the end, which is how she treats a rollback; the show isn't over, it's just not over yet. The Exchange is the last place the road opens and her last rollout. After it she lives at home in the old quarter with her troupe, the Rollouts: rehearsal at home in the morning, testing the market floor in the afternoon, the troupe's rain dance by the canal bridge in the evening, and home at night.

She was born in the old quarter, where a dancer has gone first onto every new stage, bridge and trading floor since the Exchange was a mining town. They are called canaries, and Nari is the latest. When the road began to open she left home to go first everywhere, so nobody she loves would have to. Her secret is that she is scared every single time, and the veil is so nobody sees it; a scared dancer is still a light one. Only one person has seen her face, and he has never told her. What she wants is to come home at a hundred percent. On the road she dances to Lyra's songs and always leaves before the second verse, which Lyra takes personally, and she and Explodia argue happily about how you find out whether a floor holds: by dancing on one corner of it, or by blowing it up at noon. Her soft spot is Biscuit, Mio's canary, who holds the title Rollback the Canary: Nari considers her a colleague and once sent her a very small veil.

### Neel and Hari

Neel and Hari, Blue and Green, are twins who were separated at birth at the market fair, in the rain, the way it always happens. Neel was raised in a banker's house under Kyu's tower and still clerks at the bank in the afternoons: starched, formal, and fond of the phrase "per my last message". Hari was raised by the whole old quarter and has never once been formal. They met at eighteen on the canal bridge, doing the same spin at the same moment without meaning to. Near each other, they mirror each other: when Neel types at the bank, Hari types in the air at home, and if one stubs a toe the other yelps. They cannot eat soup in the same room.

That is why they are the Rollouts' best trick. Only one of them is ever on stage, and the other waits in the wings in the same costume with the other colour of sash, already dancing every step without meaning to; when the routine changes they swap with a single spin, and if the new one stumbles, one more spin and the old one is back. Hari's flaw is that he pats his heart and says "All is well!" whatever is happening, including once with a twisted ankle, a second before he was swapped on stage. Neel's flaw is that he has been in love with Bitna since the bridge and has told no one, though Hari, who feels it, has known for years.

### Eun-ho

Eun-ho is the Shadow. He dances every show behind the curtain, in time with the real music, where nobody sees, and writes down every step where his dance and the stage's are different. He dresses all in black, speaks in whispers, and the troupe forgets he is in the room: Bitna counts heads before every show and always gets four. He grew up in the Counting House, where his parents compared the Exchange's ledgers line by line every night, and he is still happiest when two columns agree. He learned his trick from the Exchange itself: replay the day's orders into a new matching engine and compare its fills with the real ones, and you know what it would have done before anyone trades on it.

He is the best dancer of the five, and the second lead in his own life. He is the one who has seen Nari's face, at a midnight rehearsal when she thought the room was empty, and he has never told her. His notebook has every show in it, every difference down to the beat, and in the corner of every page the same note: "She smiled at bar 12." He has missed his moment to say so about four hundred times. Nari says goodnight to the curtain every night, and he has never worked out whether she knows.

### Bitna

Bitna was the lamplighter on the lane before the Cloud went down, and she still does every odd job in the old quarter: mends the market stalls, fixes the flickering lamps, settles arguments, and knows everyone's business before they do. She is the Rollouts' stage manager. The troupe learns new moves weeks early, but nobody dances one until she lights its lantern, and if a move goes wrong in the middle of a song she blows the lantern out and everyone dances the old step without missing a beat. When she finally lights a lantern she says "Go. Go and live your life," with the gravity of a father at a railway platform, and the troupe both dreads it and lives for it.

The house's back wall holds four hundred paper lanterns, one for every move the troupe has ever learned, and three hundred and eighty of them are dark and for moves nobody remembers. She will not take any down, in case. She can tell Neel and Hari apart at a glance and will not say how; it is because Neel folds his sleeves twice, and because she looks. Her soft spot is the first lantern on the wall, the one for Nari's very first step, which she has never let go dark.

### The Rollouts' side quests

Nari gives both quests, and they are data in `game/js/rollouts-quests.js`. Each step is one housemate's trouble and one system design idea. The characters are original; the films and dramas named below are inspirations only.

**Lost and Found** (`lostfound`). Nari gives it once the Exchange is open (after chapter 9) and you have met her at her house, where she hands you her last sighting gift. Until it is done her housemates are off their schedules, lost around the Exchange and the road.

1. Neel is at the hotel. He pressed BOOK once and got nine bookings. The lesson is an idempotent booking: every retry carries the same idempotency key, and the hotel answers a key it has already seen with the first booking instead of a new one.
2. Hari is busking at Lyra's stage on the North Road, because his wallet paid the diner and the diner never got the money. The lesson is a transfer as a saga: each step has a compensating step, so when the diner's side fails the wallet is paid back instead of left short.
3. Bitna is at the lamp-oil auction, outbid by a ticket that came in after hers at a higher price. The lesson is an order book with price-time priority: the better price wins, arrival only breaks ties at the same price, and a single sequencer stamps that arrival order so nobody can argue about who was first.
4. Back at the house you reconcile: count the plates and the shoes against who should be home. They come to one more than Bitna's head count, and the extra one is Eun-ho, behind the curtain. He was never lost. Nobody checked.

The payoff is the house noticeboard. Each housemate pins up a check-in that lapses unless it is renewed, a heartbeat with a TTL, which is service discovery: the house knows who is in by who has checked in lately, not by counting plates. The housemates go back to their schedules, and Nari gives you a relic, the Rollouts' Metronome: "If you play 3 or fewer cards in a turn, draw 3 extra cards at the start of your next turn." It is a gift and is never sold in a shop. Inspirations: the twins lost and found come from Judwaa and Seeta aur Geeta, Bitna at the auction is Hometown Cha-Cha-Cha's odd-jobber, Nari's chatter is Geet's from Jab We Met, and the Metronome's rule comes from a classic deckbuilder's pocket watch relic.

**The Show Isn't Over** (`showover`). Nari gives it after chapter 10, once Lost and Found is done. It is the troupe's new routine going live, and it is Nari and Eun-ho's story.

1. Bitna's lantern wall has four hundred lanterns and three hundred and eighty of them are dark. The lesson is feature flags and flag debt: retire a flag once its move is danced, and never reuse one. The nod is to Knight Capital, which in 2012 reused an old flag, woke code nobody had removed, and lost about 440 million dollars in 45 minutes.
2. Eun-ho's notebook shows he has been shadow traffic all along, dancing the new routine to the real music and writing down every difference. The lesson is telling noise from real differences, and making sure the shadow has no side effects: he never steps on stage.
3. The twins' blue-green swap. A readiness check has to test something real, not take Hari's "All is well!" for an answer. The shared mat, the schema both routines stand on, changes by expand and contract: add what the new routine needs first, and take away what the old one used only once nobody dances it. Blue stays warm in the wings, so a rollback is one spin.
4. Nari's canary goes out at one, ten and fifty percent, each stage compared with a baseline danced at the same time rather than with yesterday's show, and you call them: hold, promote, promote.
5. The Market Open finale is the hundred percent: the whole troupe in one big group dance. Bitna lights the routine's lantern and gives her blessing, "Go. Go and live your life." Nari's last line answers Eun-ho's notebook and every goodnight she said to the curtain: "Good night, Eun-ho. I know you saw. Bar twelve."

Inspirations: the title is Om Shanti Om's promise that if it isn't a happy ending, it isn't the end; Nari's hidden face is True Beauty's; Eun-ho is the second lead of Reply 1988 and Start-Up, with a Goblin-style reaper look; Hari's "All is well" is from 3 Idiots; Bitna's blessing is the strict father's at the railway platform in DDLJ; and the finale is inspired by the Naatu Naatu dance-off.

After Lost and Found the house keeps its schedule. Everyone is home in the morning and at night. In the afternoon Nari tests the market floor, Neel clerks at the bank, Hari busks at Lyra's stage on the road, Bitna tends the lamps and Eun-ho stays home. In the evening the troupe's show is beside the canal bridge in the Exchange, with Eun-ho behind a tree.

## The Eventual Isles

Backstories and relationship arcs for the islanders, in the same spirit as Pingsby's. Everything here builds on what the Isles already say (the cast titles and looks in `js/isles/isles-data.js`, the scenes in `js/isles/isles-story.js`, each island's chain and idle lines, `js/saule-quest.js` and `js/ploy-quest.js`) and agrees with the schedules in `js/folk-far.js`. Anything beyond those lines is new, and Ladi can veto it without breaking anything. The arcs are data in `isles-arcs.js`, beside this file.

One thread runs under all of them: the quiet apprentice who signs everything "K." passed through the Isles once a generation, never spoke, and left a note in every life she touched. Nobody on the Isles knows her name until the lighthouse opens. The arcs keep it that way: no scene says "Kyu" before the lighthouse chain is done.

### Harbourmaster Leilani

Leilani grew up on Ferry Landing, the daughter and granddaughter of harbourmasters. The job came with a jacket, a board and a ring of keys, one of them tagged "Lighthouse, lock two. Do not lose. K." She has never lost it, and until you arrive she has never used it. As a young clerk she worked beside the quiet apprentice who timed every crossing twice and signed the schedule at the bottom. She liked her very much and told her so far too rarely, and she still finds her notes in odd places.

What Leilani wants is one thing the whole archipelago agrees on at the same moment. That is why she wrote the letter to Pingsby, and why the bridges matter to her more than she lets on. Her soft spot is Captain Kwame. When they were seven, the ferry left on time and left him behind on the dock overnight, and she sat with him and shared her tea. She still keeps a second cup of tea for him every evening, when he comes in to file the ferry log, and after the lighthouse opens she leaves one on its step too.

By day she is at her board, with an afternoon on the harbour square, and she spends the night in the harbour office.

### Captain Kwame

Kwame has captained the ferry for as long as anyone can say "late", and he has never once been on time. He calls it a feature. The truth is softer. The night he was left on the dock as a boy, he decided no ferry of his would ever leave anyone behind, so he waits for the last running passenger, the last parcel and, once, the last turtle. He still sails by the original schedule, the one with "ish" added in the margin in tidy handwriting, and he writes his logbook in the format from before the storm, which Lúcia keeps a whole dictionary for.

He likes to say a captain should arrive last, and he likes even more that Leilani is always there when he does. He files the ferry log at the harbour office every evening, drinks her tea, and sleeps aboard. He will tell you he is eventually punctual. He is exactly as late every day, to the minute, and he is quietly proud of it.

### Saule Apai

Saule came from the steppe far to the east, where the sky is so big that you learn to count things properly: sheep, stars, late ferries. Her grandfather hunted with an eagle, her aunt could lift the yurt by herself, and her cousin went to the same yurt school as Aigerim's mother. She is strict, warm, and has marked Kwame late forty days in a row. Everyone calls her Apai in the end, even the Harbourmaster.

As a girl she was the one who asked "But why?" until the grown-ups said "Because." She has never said "Because" to a child. Her secret is the Why Book, a fat notebook with a page for every question her pupils have ever asked, and a ribbon on each one she hasn't answered yet. She keeps one gradebook per subject, and she counts the Why Book as a subject. She teaches inside the school in the morning, takes her milk tea out on the path in front of the school in the afternoon, and goes home at night.

### The children of the Isles School

Three small students, aged six to nine, who raise their hands before anyone has finished a sentence. They ask why the ferry is late, how long "eventually" is (is it longer than lunch?), and why the sea is salty. One wants to be a ferry when they grow up, because ferries never have to hurry. One has a sister and a very loud cat. They play "Incident" at break, refuse to rub anything out of their workbooks since Mang Tomas visited, and draw moustaches on Uptima's poster.

They are the Isles' teach-back: whatever you fix, they ask you to explain it to them, and they cheer when it makes sense. They want to know everything, and their soft spot is Saule Apai, who never tells them to stop asking. Mornings they are in class, in the evening they play on the harbour square, and at night they are home in bed.

### Madame Farida

Farida learnt her trade as a girl apprenticed to the market ledger, where she once watched a quiet woman balance a whole year's books in one evening by candlelight and sign it "K." She has run the Bank of the Isles ever since on one rule: balances must balance, and everything else is decoration. She is precise, dry, and very polite to Old Ruth in the way that makes the bank chilly. Her clerks Amira and Bashir are honest, fast, and now write `balance = balance + 50` for every deposit.

She calls Admiral Mutex a hammer that sees every ship as a nail, and she has been bringing him her mother's ma'amoul for years, anonymously, in a tin. He locks them away and has never eaten one. She is in the bank all afternoon and counts the vault again by lamplight at night.

### Admiral Mutex

The Admiral kept the Ledger's harbour safe for forty years with one rule: one ship at a time. It started with the Great Omelette, when he was a young officer who waved two egg boats into the same berth at the same moment. Nobody was hurt. Nothing else was ever the same. Since then he has locked the harbour, the vault door, the bakery on principle, the tide once (it took three days to get the sea back), his wardrobe, his moustache wax and his biscuit tin, whose key is inside the biscuit tin.

Under the moustache he is gentle and a little lonely. After you clear his harbour queue he is practising "a lock for the rows that need it", and he fishes on Sundays from a rock Gary offered him. He stands watch over the harbour in the afternoon and goes home at night. He has always known who leaves the ma'amoul.

### Mang Tomas

Tomas has run the Log Mill for forty years on a design signed "K.": the sheds in a row, the basket, the logbook, and underneath, "Newest first. Always." He has never edited a plank; a change is a new plank, and the old one leaves at the night merge "like a polite guest". The logbook lives in a tin box, because he is not a monster. He calls everyone "anak", visits the school to talk about planks, and bakes, which is how he ended up both invited and uninvited to the Twin Festival.

His soft spot is Plank #1, superseded eleven times and still his favourite, and Bloom. He watches the sunset from the shore below the mill in the evening and merges in the mill at night. He doesn't mind being tired. He minds customers waiting.

### Bloom

Bloom is the fourth dog called Bloom, and wears the same red collar as the other three. The first Bloom arrived forty years ago as a puppy in a fish basket from Ruth's market, carried by a quiet woman who tucked a note in the collar, "Ask the dog first. K.", and left. Every Bloom since has been certain, beyond any argument, about where a plank is not. About where a plank might be, Bloom is an optimist: the third shed, a tree, a seagull, a handbag.

Bloom wants snacks, the sunset and Mang Tomas, in roughly that order, and sleeps across all four shed doors during compaction, which should not be physically possible. The school measured Bloom's power level at over nine thousand. He wags at every footstep in case it is Tomas.

### Professora Lúcia

Lúcia keeps three dictionaries for every island (yesterday's, today's and tomorrow's) in the Tower of Tongues, plus one just for Kwame, and one that turned out to be a sandwich. The first dictionary in the tower is signed "K." and says "Add, never reuse" on page one, underlined nine times in three colours. She is dramatic, generous and genuinely moved when an old letter still makes sense. "Querido" is for everyone.

She wants to search every letter ever written on the Isles, typos especially, because typos are the most romantic part. Her soft spot is Cartographer Minh, who writes her letters with no words in them, only maps. She is in the tower in the morning and at night, and takes the air on the path down to the pier in the evening.

### Cartographer Minh

Minh knows where everything goes; that, and selling pencils, is the whole job. He mapped the ferry route twice because each Twin mayor wanted their own copy, and keeps more drawers than he has maps so a new cabinet can take whole drawers. He asks every customer what they will look for before he decides where anything goes. Most of them leave, and the good ones come back. He sold Kaito a pencil instead of a Kubernetes, and his reading glasses are on his head.

Long ago he reused the anchor symbol on a map to mean "café", and three ships docked at a café. He has not reused a symbol since, and Lúcia has not let him forget it. His soft spot is the one drawer in the shop with no label, full of her letters. They are in a different format every time and he keeps every one. He opens the shop in the morning, walks down by the pier in the evening and sleeps over the shop.

### Mayor Ha-jin

The younger twin, by four minutes, and mayor of West Twin. Ha-jin is dry, stubborn and fair. He believes the stage belongs on the hill, because the beach floods at high tide, and that four minutes is nothing, barely a ferry delay, which makes them concurrent. He agrees with his sister more often than he will say, especially about clerks who keep whichever plan was saved last.

His soft spot is his sister. Each year he plans her a birthday surprise, and each year it is somehow the same day as hers. He works in his hall in the afternoon and at night, and comes out to argue across the water in the morning and evening.

### Mayor Ha-eun

The elder twin and mayor of East Twin. Ha-eun is quick, warm and absolutely right: the stage belongs on the beach, which is a fact with a sea view. She likes to think four minutes makes her the leader. She invited Mang Tomas to the festival, and was furious when the merge uninvited him after he had already baked the cake.

The twins were elected on the same day by the same number of votes, and they have kept their own lantern tallies ever since, the only sum they have never argued about. Her soft spot is her brother, and she keeps his childhood drawing of the two islands with a bridge between them, which nobody had built yet. Her hours mirror his: her own hall in the afternoon and at night, the shore in the morning and evening.

### Old Ruth

Ruth has counted the fish on the market since she was six. That is eighty years, and she has never lost one, though she puts one in the wrong hour constantly. When she was six, a quiet young woman with a notebook stood beside her at the stall and, without a word, showed her how to count by the tag and not the clock. The front of the market ledger still says "Count by the tag, not the clock. Eventually is good enough for fish. K."

Ruth is brisk, funny, calls you "pet", gave Kwame his own column on the board ("later"), and sends Farida a tuna when she loses an argument. Her secret is a reply she wrote at six and never sent: a tally slip, folded small, that has lived in her apron pocket for eighty years. She counts in the market by morning and evening, works the far end of the market floor in the afternoon, and goes home at night.

### The Keeper

Never seen, only read. The lighthouse has been sealed since before anyone remembers, and the three lights come on every night, in step. The door is covered in notes in small, patient handwriting, signed "K.": "Lights in step. Back in five minutes." "Please do not knock. I cannot come to the door." "Gulls: please stop." It is the same hand as the margins of your Cheat Sheet, the ferry schedule, the mill plans, the bank's books and the market ledger.

The lighthouse chain reveals who she is: Kyu, Uptima's apprentice, who kept the lights in agreement for ten thousand years and wrote because when she speaks, people only hear static. Her notes were never really instructions. They were her talking, and in ten thousand years nobody wrote back until you did. What she wants is for nobody to notice, because that means the job was done. Her soft spot is everyone on the Isles, each of whom she helped once and quietly. Since the door opened she answers notes with a small, neat tick.

### Ploy

Ploy founded the Tide Pool Game Club, made herself president and made up the rules, usually halfway through a game. She is Thai, loud, sincere and an excellent detective about anything except the wind. Every game ends in a victory dance, whoever wins, because there are no losers in the Game Club, only dancers. She invented "last write wins" by accident ("I called it loud") and gives very good interview tips.

What she wants is for the club to be real, and since Game Night it is: five members, five signatures, and an append-only scoreboard by order of the president. Her soft spot is Meron, who reads her rules back to her ("I love her. Don't tell her"), and, secretly, you. She is the only member who never goes home: the yard by day, and game night and rule-writing in the clubhouse until late.

### Nok

Nok is Ploy's eternal rival, self-appointed, and has been a member since the day he lost his first game to her. He is green, loud and burning with youth. He runs laps of the island when his feelings get too big, has lapped Kwame's ferry nine times, and drinks Meron's bitter training tea and asks for more. The score between him and Ploy is forty-nine all, or fifty to forty-nine, because the chalkboard got rained on.

He wants a record nobody can rub out, and he got one; he has read it start to finish four times and cries at the same parts. His soft spot is losing magnificently. He is in the yard by day, in the clubhouse in the evening, and home at night.

### Kai

Kai knows how every game ends before it starts. It is exhausting. The only thing he cannot see coming is Ploy changing the rules, which is why he comes every day. He is deadpan, polite, says "good grief", and will tell you nothing about the future for a plate of mango sticky rice, which is the best thing he can offer anyone.

Everyone on the Isles thinks loudly except Meron, whose head is a quiet alphabetical list of plants, so he stands beside her when he needs a rest. He thinks about the third spin of Ploy's victory dance a great deal. He keeps the club's one surprise to himself whenever there is one, because surprises are rare and he likes other people to have them. He spends the day in the yard and the evening in the clubhouse, and goes home at night.

### Arjun

Arjun is the club treasurer. The club has eleven shells, and he counts them every hour in case one escapes. Awake, he is terrified of crabs, goddesses, taxes and Meron's coffee. Asleep, he speaks in calm, perfect sentences about sharding and snapshots, and he won Game Night with forty-one wins, all of them asleep. He does not remember any of them.

He wants, very badly, to be brave, and he is braver than he knows, because he keeps coming back. His soft spot is the treasury. He spends the day in the yard under his umbrella and the evening in the clubhouse, and he sleeps at home, or wherever he happens to be.

### Meron

Meron is the club's referee, herbalist and taster of everything. She grew up in her grandmother's coffee house in Addis, where coffee is served three times (abol, tona, baraka) and where, she says, the records were better than the club's even though half the customers were goats. She reads every rule Ploy invents and reads it back, tests anything before she signs it, licks ink to see what it's made of, and could hear colours until lunch after eating a pink flower by the school.

She brews Nok bitter tea, offers Arjun coffee "not today", and lets Kai stand by her. Ploy has offered her vice president, and she is considering it, a mango at a time. Her soft spot is Ploy, which she would deny with evidence. She gathers herbs on the mainland path in the morning, plays in the clubhouse in the evening and goes home at night.
